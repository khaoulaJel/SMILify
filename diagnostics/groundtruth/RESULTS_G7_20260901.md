## The result

**Anatomical accuracy is nearly free.**

- **λ = 0.01: joint error falls 38.9% → 27.5%, a 29% improvement, for a 19% increase in chamfer.**
- λ = 0.03: **17.6%**, less than half production's error, at 1.54× chamfer.
- λ = 0.1: **8.99%**, a 77% improvement, at 2.29× chamfer — and this *beats* G6's joints-only
  plausible oracle (11.01%), because here the surface term is helping rather than being ignored.

The pre-registered knee criterion (best joint residual at ≤ 2× production chamfer) returns **17.61%
at λ = 0.03** — 45% of production's error, comfortably inside the 60% bar.

**COMPATIBLE.** The surface data does not oppose anatomical correctness. G6 established that the
objective contains no term constraining joint *position*; G7 shows that adding one costs almost
nothing in surface fit. **This is a well-posed, cheap fix, and it is the first intervention in this
investigation with a large positive effect.**

It also explains, retrospectively, why the entire Z-series returned nulls: every one of those
experiments reweighted or constrained terms that already existed. None added the missing one, and
no reweighting can constrain a quantity the objective never mentions.

## What this is NOT, stated plainly

**This is a demonstration of headroom, not a deployable method.** The joint targets here are the
human annotations. At inference there are none. The result says: *if approximate joint positions can
be supplied, the fit improves dramatically at little cost in surface fit.* Supplying them is the
engineering problem this licenses, not one it solves.

**The joint residual is measured on the same joints used as targets.** That is inherent to an oracle
experiment and it means the number demonstrates *reachability*, not *generalisation*. The
distinction matters and is testable — see below.

**Chamfer does get worse**, by 1.19–2.29×. Whether that is a real cost depends on what chamfer is
for. G1 and G2 showed surface metrics do not predict anatomical accuracy, so trading surface fit for
anatomy is likely a good trade — but that should be demonstrated on the morphometric endpoints, not
assumed.

## The two experiments this makes urgent

**1. Held-out joints — does the constraint generalise anatomically?** Fit with only a subset of
joints as targets (say the body axis and proximal leg joints) and measure the residual on the
*held-out* joints. If constraining some joints also fixes the others, the model is being pulled into
the right anatomical configuration and a *partial* or *approximate* skeleton is enough. If the
held-out joints do not improve, the term is memorising its targets and a predicted skeleton would
have to be near-complete and near-exact to help. **This single test decides whether the fix is
practical**, and it needs no new annotation.

**2. Does it move the morphometrics?** Refit with λ ≈ 0.03 and recompute G2's per-block
reliability against ground truth. The entire point of the pipeline is trustworthy measurements; a
77% reduction in joint error that leaves trait reliability at R ≈ 0 would be a hollow win.

## The deployment path, if both hold

A predicted skeleton is sufficient — it need not be a human one. 3D joint prediction from meshes or
images is exactly what OmniTrax provides for pose in this ecosystem, and the 14 annotated specimens
are the seed of a supervised set. That closes the loop with the WOLO/replicAnt line rather than
opening a new front, and it is the concrete engineering consequence of this result.
