# Z13 — subfamily does NOT change the deliverable. The high accuracy is class imbalance.

Same specimens, same features (traits R ≥ 0.1 + betas), same lot-blind protocol, both taxonomic
levels. 492 specimens, 9 subfamilies, 56 genera, 31 lots.

## The result, and the trap in it

| level | classes | model | top-1 | top-3 | lift | macro-recall |
|---|---:|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| subfamily | 9 | LDA shrinkage | **0.581** | 0.866 | **1.52×** | 0.288 |
| subfamily | 9 | logistic L2 | 0.541 | 0.864 | 1.60× | 0.299 |
| genus | 56 | LDA shrinkage | 0.217 | 0.392 | **6.25×** | 0.115 |
| genus | 56 | logistic L2 | 0.217 | 0.378 | 5.90× | 0.117 |

**58.1% subfamily accuracy is not the win it looks like.** Myrmicinae is 231 of 492 specimens, so
**predicting "Myrmicinae" for everything scores 47.0%**. The classifier achieves 58.1% — a real but
modest **+11.1 points over a constant predictor**.

The lift column says the same thing from the other side: **1.52× at subfamily against 6.25× at
genus.** The *signal* is four times stronger at genus level; subfamily merely looks better because
its class structure is easy. Macro-recall confirms it — 0.288 means performance is concentrated in
the one dominant subfamily and poor across the other eight.

**Note the permutation null is the wrong comparator here.** At 0.383 it sits *below* the 0.470
majority-class rate, because a classifier fitted to permuted labels learns noise and underperforms
a constant predictor. For an imbalanced problem the majority-class rate is the honest baseline, and
that is what the +11.1 points is measured against.

## Nest-mate agreement, both levels

| level / model | same answer | chance | lift | both correct |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| subfamily, LDA | 50.6% | 35.6% | 1.42× | 37.6% |
| genus, LDA | 23.1% | 4.6% | **5.07×** | 12.9% |

50.6% subfamily agreement again dissolves against a 35.6% chance rate. Two nest-mates agree on
subfamily half the time, and a third of that agreement is available from the class prior alone.

## Verdict

**The deliverable does not change category.** Subfamily is not a level at which SMILify "makes an
automated coarse identification a specialist can act on" — it is a level at which the class
distribution is skewed enough to flatter any classifier. This was the pre-registered branch: the
ceiling is a property of the **measurement**, not of class granularity. Coarsening the taxonomy
does not recover signal that is not there.

The write-up should say this explicitly, because "58% subfamily accuracy" is exactly the number
that would otherwise be quoted as a success.

## One number that is not comparable to Z12, and why

Genus top-1 reads **0.217** here against Z12's 0.179. That is not an improvement — this analysis is
restricted to specimens carrying a subfamily mapping, which leaves 56 genera and 492 specimens
instead of 83 and 602. Fewer classes is an easier problem. The two are the same pipeline on
different subsets; only the within-table subfamily-vs-genus comparison is like-for-like, which is
why both levels were re-run on identical specimens here rather than quoting Z12's figure.

84 genera have no subfamily mapping in `taxonomy.py`. Extending that table would let this run at
full corpus, but on this evidence it would not change the conclusion.
